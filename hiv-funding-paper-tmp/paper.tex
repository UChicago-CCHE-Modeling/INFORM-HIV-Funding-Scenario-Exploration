\begin{abstract}
Funding for HIV prevention and treatment in the United States faces growing uncertainty, and sustained reductions could reverse decades of progress toward HIV elimination. We used INFORM-HIV, an agent-based network model calibrated to HIV prevalence among Black men who have sex with men (MSM) in Chicago, to project how reductions in HIV program funding would affect HIV transmission in this population over the next decade. Reductions in treatment (ART) and prevention (PrEP) funding were projected to produce substantial increases in HIV incidence. A 25\% reduction in ART funding increased projected 10-year HIV incidence by approximately [X]\%, whereas a 25\% reduction in [PrEP / both ART and PrEP] increased incidence by [Y]\%. In a setting where HIV incidence is already rising in some populations, these findings underscore the importance of sustained funding for HIV prevention and treatment for the United States to successfully eliminate HIV over the next decade.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Despite advances in HIV prevention and treatment, Black men who have sex with men (MSM) continue to experience disproportionately high rates of HIV infection in the United States~\cite{mayer_persistent_2021, sullivan_epidemiology_2021, beyrer_call_2021}. Many US states and jurisdictions have launched initiatives to eliminate HIV over the next decade~\cite{sullivan_epidemiology_2021, beyrer_call_2021} by expanding biomedical prevention and reducing barriers to engagement and retention in care~\cite{grant_uptake_2014, Fed_HIV_stats, CDCPrEP2022}. These initiatives emphasize approaches tailored to priority populations, including Black MSM, and focus on addressing the social determinants of health that interfere with prevention and care engagement~\cite{mayer_persistent_2021, schneider_at_2017, sullivan_epidemiology_2021}. Illinois launched Getting to Zero Illinois (GTZ-IL) to end HIV in the state by 2030~\cite{chicago_getting_to_zero, chicago_gtz_2.0}, combining expanded biomedical prevention with efforts to reduce structural and system-level barriers to care.

Recent shifts in US government priorities have raised the prospect of reductions in federal and state HIV program funding, which could reduce access to essential prevention and treatment services. Local policy makers must decide how to use existing programs and infrastructure to sustain progress toward HIV elimination amid changing epidemiological trends and uncertain future funding. Mathematical models can support these decisions by projecting how funding changes might alter HIV transmission across a range of plausible assumptions and future scenarios, particularly valuable when resources are scarce and the future is uncertain. In this study, we examined how reductions in HIV prevention and treatment funding could affect HIV transmission among Black MSM in Chicago. Guided by input from local GTZ-IL stakeholders, we used an agent-based model (ABM) calibrated to the current Chicago epidemic to simulate a range of antiretroviral therapy (ART) and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) funding scenarios over the next 10 years, and assessed their effects on HIV incidence and prevalence.

\subsection{Model Structure}
\label{sec:methods-model-structure}
INFORM-HIV is a stochastic, discrete-time, agent-based network model of HIV transmission among Black MSM aged 16--34 in Chicago, Illinois. Each simulated individual (``agent'') has demographic, behavioral, and clinical attributes that evolve over time. The formation and dissolution of main and casual sexual partnerships are represented as dynamic networks using separable temporal exponential-family random graph models (STERGMs). The probability of HIV transmission within a partnership depends on viral load, disease stage, condom use, and PrEP use, and social and economic factors further shape PrEP use and engagement in HIV care. The model was parameterized to represent the Chicago epidemic using data from 2013 through 2023, reflecting local rates of HIV incidence and prevalence, care engagement, and PrEP use at the end of 2023. Data on sociodemographics, sexual networks, and behavior came from the uConnect study, a longitudinal study of young Black MSM conducted in Chicago between 2013 and 2015.\cite{schneider_at_2017, schneider_skaathun_2017, khanna_preexposure_2016} An earlier version of the model has been described previously \cite{khanna_modeling_2019}; further details on design and parameterization are provided in the supplementary appendix.

\subsection{Model Calibration}
\label{sec:methods-calibration}

\subsubsection{Parameter space reduction via Morris Screening} 
Many model parameters are uncertain because empirical data are limited or not directly translatable into parameter estimates. We specified plausible ranges for these parameters from empirical data and available literature (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:morris_params}). Since a full sensitivity analysis across all uncertain parameters was computationally infeasible, we prioritized parameters that were most uncertain (those with limited or imprecise supporting data) and those expected to most strongly influence outcomes (e.g., transmission probability), yielding 21 parameters for screening. We then used the Morris screening method \cite{morris1991factorial, campolongo2007effective} to reduce dimensionality by identifying the most influential of these 21 inputs, yielding 6 parameters for calibration. Morris screening ranks parameters by their influence on a model output while accounting for nonlinearities and interactions; full methodological detail is provided in the supplementary appendix.

% Briefly, the approach perturbs each parameter one at a time across a discretized grid of the input space, while randomly sampling the remaining parameters within their respective ranges. For each parameter \(x_i\), the corresponding elementary effect is computed as
% \begin{equation}
%     EE_i = \frac{Y(\mathbf{x}_{1}, \ldots, x_i + \Delta, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_{k}) - Y(\mathbf{x})}{\Delta},
% \end{equation}
% where \(Y(\cdot)\) denotes the model output of interest (in our case, the average HIV prevalance), and \(\Delta\) is a predetermined step size within the normalized \([0,1]\) input space. Repeating this process over multiple random trajectories yields a distribution of elementary effects for each parameter. Two summary statistics are typically derived: (i) the mean of the absolute elementary effects, \(\mu^*_i\), which reflects the overall influence of parameter \(i\) on the output, and (ii) the standard deviation, \(\sigma_i\), which captures nonlinearity and interaction effects. Parameters with large \(\mu^*_i\) are considered highly influential, whereas those with large \(\sigma_i\) indicate complex effects or interactions.

% We implemented the Morris design using \(r = 20\) trajectories and \(p = 6\) grid levels. Based on the joint examination of \(\mu^*_i\) and \(\sigma_i\) values, seven parameters were identified as key drivers of the model outputs and retained for subsequent calibration. The selected parameters and their ranges are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:morris_params}, while Figure~\ref{fig:morris_results} shows the corresponding sensitivity indices.

\subsubsection{Calibration to baseline epidemic}

After reducing the parameter space to the six most influential parameters, we calibrated the model to estimated HIV prevalence among Black MSM aged 15--34 in Chicago at the end of 2023, the most recent data available at the time. \cite{idph_msm_plwhiv_chicago_2024} We calibrated the model to HIV prevalence rather than incidence because prevalence tends to be more stable and less sensitive to short-term fluctuations. The baseline (no-change) scenario represents a static snapshot of the epidemic at a single time point, assuming a stationary baseline and a well-mixed population, which is sufficient for the goal of comparing the relative impacts of different strategies for a given baseline. However, more recent trends in incidence may not be captured.

Rather than a single best-fitting parameter set, calibration produced a distribution over plausible parameterizations, from which we retained 100 calibrated model states (``checkpoints''), each reflecting a plausible representation of the epidemic at the end of the calibration period. Propagating this ensemble forward captures uncertainty from the input parameters constrained by the data. When paired with scenario exploration via surrogates (described below), the forward propagation from the checkpoints also enables tracking the surrogate uncertainty along with the parameter uncertainty. The combination of checkpoints and surrogate assisted exploration facilitates a far wider range of scenarios to explore than a full factorial simulation design would allow given the model's computational cost. Full calibration details are provided in the supplementary appendix.

We make the assumption that coverage increases linearly with government spending, while accounting for two important structural features of the HIV care system. First, some individuals may obtain ART or PrEP from non-federally funded sources (e.g., through private insurance). We represent these groups by the fractions $\gamma_{\text{ART}}$ and $\gamma_{\text{PrEP}}$, which capture the proportion of MSM whose access is not directly affected by changes in public funding. Second, program delivery is imperfect, and losses can arise from delayed linkage to care, imperfect adherence, and administrative or operational challenges. These effects are captured through scaling factors $\alpha$ for ART and $\beta$ for PrEP that adjust how efficiently spending translates into coverage.

Putting these components together, coverage is represented as:

\begin{align}
P_{\text{ART}} &= \min\!\left(1,\;
    \frac{G_{\text{ART}}}{\alpha\, C_{\text{ART}}\, N_{\text{HIV+}}}
    + \gamma_{\text{ART}}\right), \label{eq:PART} \\[6pt]
P_{\text{PrEP}} &= \min\!\left(1,\;
    \frac{G_{\text{PrEP}}}{\beta\, C_{\text{PrEP}}\, N_{\text{HIV-}}}
    + \gamma_{\text{PrEP}}\right), \label{eq:PPrEP}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Simulation of ART and PrEP Scenarios}

To evaluate scenarios while accounting for parameter uncertainty, we ran forward simulations from the 100 calibrated model states (i.e., checkpoints). We used each of the 100 checkpoints (Section~\ref{sec:methods-calibration}) as the initial condition for a 10-year projection under a specified pair of proportional reductions in ART and PrEP coverage, $(r_{\mathrm{ART}}, r_{\mathrm{PrEP}})$. Repeating this across all 100 checkpoints yields, for each scenario, a distribution of projected incidence and prevalence trajectories that reflects uncertainty in the calibrated parameters and epidemic state.

Simulating a fine grid of $(r_{\mathrm{ART}}, r_{\mathrm{PrEP}})$ combinations directly would be computationally prohibitive, since resource use scales with both the number of scenarios and the number of checkpoints. We therefore used surrogate models to approximate these projections efficiently (below).

\subsubsection{Surrogate assisted exploration}

A surrogate model is a statistical approximation of the simulator that, once trained, predicts outcomes for new intervention settings without rerunning the full model. For each of the 100 checkpoints, we trained Gaussian process surrogates \cite{gramacy_surrogates_2020} to map a pair of reductions $(r_{\mathrm{ART}}, r_{\mathrm{PrEP}})$ to the projected 10-year incidence and prevalence trajectories (a separate surrogate for each outcome). Training data came from simulating 20 intervention combinations, each with 10 stochastic replicates, at every checkpoint (20 combinations $\times$ 10 replicates $\times$ 100 checkpoints $=$ 20{,}000 simulations). Because a separate surrogate is trained at each checkpoint, predictions for any intervention combination can be pooled across the 100 checkpoints to give a predictive distribution for 10-year incidence and prevalence that reflects both calibrated parameter uncertainty and the approximation introduced by the surrogates. Further details are provided in the supplementary appendix.

\subsection{Statistical Analyses}
\label{sec:methods-stat-analyses}
Using the 100 checkpoint-trained surrogates, we predicted incidence and prevalence across a 101-point Latin hypercube design over the coverage-reduction space (0 to $-75\%$ for each of ART and PrEP), and performed a linear transformation (Section~\ref{sec:methods-mapping-funding}) mapping (ART x PrEP) coverage reductions to combinations of (ART x PrEP) funding reduction scenarios, by 1\% increments respectively. We report 95\% posterior predictive intervals for HIV incidence and prevalence in each funding scenario.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

In the baseline scenario model-projected HIV incidence at 10 years was 4.19 per 100 person-years (95\% credible interval [CrI] 3.16--5.06; Table~\ref{tab:funding_scenarios}). We compared each scenario relative to this baseline and computed mean HIV incidence, the absolute increase in incidence (cases per 100 person-years), and an incidence rate ratio (IRR), each with a 95\% CrI. We first report scenarios that reduce antiretroviral therapy (ART) or pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) use directly (Figure~\ref{fig:forest_incidence_risk_ratio_funding} A), then scenarios where we estimate a reduction in ART or PrEP use driven by reductions in government funding (Figure~\ref{fig:forest_incidence_risk_ratio_funding} B).

Reducing ART or PrEP use resulted in increases in HIV incidence, with reductions in ART driving the largest increases for a single percentage point use. For example, a 10\% reduction in ART use raised incidence to 5.28 per 100 person-years (4.10--6.18), an absolute increase of 1.08 cases per 100 person-years (0.75--1.44) and an IRR of 1.26 (1.18--1.34). A 25\% reduction raised incidence to 6.98 per 100 person-years (5.56--8.05; IRR 1.67, 1.54--1.82), and a 40\% reduction raised it to 8.58 per 100 person-years (7.11--9.68; IRR 2.06, 1.86--2.28). Reducing PrEP use had a smaller effect at each level: a 10\% reduction yielded an IRR of 1.06 (0.99--1.14), a 25\% reduction an IRR of 1.16 (1.04--1.31), and a 40\% reduction an IRR of 1.25 (1.08--1.48), corresponding to an incidence of 5.26 per 100 person-years (3.89--6.51). Reducing both ART and PrEP use by 40\% simultaneously produced the largest impact of any scenario, increasing incidence to 10.12 per 100 person-years (8.46--11.65), an absolute increase of 5.92 cases per 100 person-years (4.88--7.18) and an IRR of 2.43 (2.13--2.78).

Reductions in government funding produced smaller increases in HIV incidence than the equivalent percentage reductions in use, because a funding cut of a given fraction lowers coverage by less than that fraction since not all ART or PrEP use is government-funded(Figure~\ref{fig:forest_incidence_risk_ratio_funding}). A 10\% ART funding reduction mapped to a 7.5\% reduction in ART use, raising incidence to 5.00 per 100 person-years (3.85--5.89; IRR 1.19, 1.13--1.26); 25\% and 40\% ART funding reductions mapped to 18.9\% and 30.2\% use reductions and IRRs of 1.50 (1.39--1.63) and 1.81 (1.65--1.98), respectively. The dampening was most pronounced for PrEP. A 40\% reduction in both ART and PrEP funding raised incidence to \bothFortyIncidenceBaseline\ per 100 person-years (\bothFortyIncidenceBaselineLo--\bothFortyIncidenceBaselineHi), an absolute increase of \bothFortyAbsBaseline\ cases per 100 person-years (\bothFortyAbsBaselineLo--\bothFortyAbsBaselineHi) and an IRR of \bothFortyIRRBaseline\ (\bothFortyIRRBaselineLo--\bothFortyIRRBaselineHi), below the IRR of 2.43 for the equivalent 40\% reduction in use.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our findings indicate that reductions in HIV program funding could substantially increase HIV transmission among Black MSM in Chicago. Even modest reductions in ART resulted in meaningful increases in transmission over 10 years. A 10\% reduction in ART resulted in a 26\% increase in HIV incidence relative to the baseline scenario, while a 25\% reduction in ART resulted in a 67\% increase in HIV incidence in the tenth year. In a population of 5,000 Black MSM, this translates to 54 and 139 new infections per year, respectively. Reductions in PrEP use also resulted in increased incidence, though to a lesser extent than ART, which is likely due at least in part to assumptions about PrEP adherence and delivery strategies. The impact of PrEP reductions would be expected to be smaller when ART coverage is maintained, and to become increasingly important in scenarios where where incidence is high or viral suppression is low. These results underscore the critical role of sustained program funding in preventing HIV transmission. 

Our findings are consistent with recent studies have demonstrated the potential impact at the national level of reductions in PrEP \cite{sullivan_excess_2025} and Ryan White program funding \citep{forster_potential_2025}.

Reductions in federal funding could result in increased disparities as those who rely on publicly funded programs for medication will be most heavily impacted.[cite] The drivers of ongoing HIV transmission are complex and rooted in social and economic inequity. HIV is increasingly concentrated in communities with the fewest resources and the least access to prevention and care. As biomedical interventions have reduced HIV in some subgroups, elimination will increasingly depend on reaching smaller, higher-need segments of the population, an effort that becomes more difficult and costly as that population grows. Sustained funding for biomedical prevention and treatment is therefore necessary but not sufficient: progress also depends on addressing housing, mental health, and substance use, all of which could be affected by future funding decisions. Reductions in funding for these supportive services, alongside direct cuts to HIV programs, could act synergistically to accelerate transmission.

Short-term cost-savings due to reductions in HIV prevention funding could be offset by increased healthcare costs long-term. It may take a long time to get back to where we were in terms of downward trajectories if essential program infrastructure is removed. 

Increases in new HIV diagnoses have occurred in Chicago \citep{chicago_hiv_data_brief_2024} and other areas (cites) and funding reductions could exacerbate or accelerate these trends. 

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion-limitations}

Several limitations should be considered. We assumed a simple linear mapping between funding reductions and changes in ART and PrEP use. In reality this relationship is likely more complex, as funds may be shifted from other programs to ensure access to HIV treatment for as long as possible. We also did not model reductions in funding for social services such as housing, which could accompany cuts to direct HIV services. For these reasons, the overall impact on the epidemic may be underestimated.

The relative effects of reductions in ART and PrEP depend on baseline coverage and adherence. Because baseline PrEP and ART coverage differ, an identical percentage reduction does not translate into the same absolute change in the number of people using each. ART coverage is also comparatively high at baseline, so reducing it by a given percentage has a larger effect than the same percentage reduction for PrEP. Direct comparisons of the relative impact of ART versus PrEP reductions should therefore be made with caution; the results are best interpreted as a range of plausible outcomes for a given funding reduction, depending on how cuts are distributed across programs.

Finally, our findings may not generalize to other populations or settings, and could be more or less optimistic depending on the magnitude of funding cuts and the availability of alternative funding.

Overall rates of reliance on government funding are likely underestimated for Black MSM.
