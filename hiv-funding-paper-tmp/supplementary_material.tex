The design, parameterization, and processes for the Integrated Framework for Modeling Social Determinants of HIV (INFORM-HIV) agent-based model have been previously published. [cite preprint] This Supplementary Appendix describes the calibration process, experimental design, and methods for mapping reductions in HIV program funding to reductions in ART and PrEP use. 

% \section{Purpose}
% \label{sec:supp-purpose}

% The INFORM-HIV model was designed to simulate HIV transmission among Black men who have sex with men (MSM) in Chicago and to evaluate interventions to reduce HIV transmission. The model facilitates evaluation of the potential impact of combinations of biomedical and structural interventions for reducing HIV transmission under varying assumptions about the underlying causal structures and uncertainties in the model. We updated and re-calibrated a previous version of the model \cite{khanna_modeling_2019} to reflect new data, changes in the HIV epidemic over time, and additional parameters. Thus, while the overall model structure is similar to the previous version, some processes and underlying data sources may have changed from the previous model version.

% \section{Introduction}
% \label{sec:supp-introduction}

%  INFORM-HIV is a stochastic, discrete-time, agent-based network model (ABNM) that represents Black MSM between the ages of 16 and 34 in the city of Chicago. This population was selected because they have historically been a key population of focus for interventions given the disproportionate impact of HIV on this community. The model was parameterized to represent the HIV epidemic in Chicago based on clinical and behavioral data collected between 2013 and 2023 and reflects local rates of HIV incidence and prevalence, care engagement, and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) use. The simulation proceeds in discrete daily time steps and HIV is transmitted through sexual contact with main and casual partners. Separable temporal exponential-family random graph models (STERGMs) are used to simulate main and casual sexual networks dynamically over time.

% \section{Overview of Model Processes}
% \label{sec:supp-overview-processes}

% Agents enter the model at age 16 and leave the model either by aging out at age 35 or according to local age-specific mortality rates that can be modified by HIV disease progression. At the start of the simulation, agents are probabilistically assigned characteristics (e.g., age, sexual roles, housing status, employment status) that affect their behaviors, partnership dynamics, and probability of acquiring HIV. Age-stratified HIV prevalence rates were assigned at the beginning of the simulation such that the model prevalence matched local prevalence estimates for young Black MSM in Chicago at the end of 2023 based on surveillance data (approximately 29.4\%). \cite{idph_msm_plwhiv_chicago_2024}

% Main and casual network connections are established at the start of the simulation and evolve over time to match the average relationship durations and degree distributions for main and casual sex partners based on local data. Partnered agents have sex at a specified frequency and use condoms for a proportion of those sex acts. HIV is transmitted through condomless anal sex between serodiscordant partners, and the probability of transmission can be modified by condom use, sexual roles, stage of infection and viral load of the HIV positive partner, and PrEP use by the HIV negative partner.

% Rates of HIV testing, diagnosis, linkage to care, viral suppression, and PrEP use were assigned to reflect current characteristics of the local HIV epidemic in Chicago. A small proportion of agents are never tested and if they acquire HIV remain undiagnosed and untreated throughout the simulation. Otherwise, HIV negative agents are tested for HIV at specified intervals and, if they acquire HIV, are diagnosed and initiate ART after a specified time interval that reflects the average time from diagnosis to ART initiation in the population. Agents who have initiated ART have a probability of being virally suppressed, updated at 6-month intervals, which reflects adherence to medication and varies according to age, time since infection, and SDOH. Agents without HIV can take PrEP for a specified duration of time with a specified level of adherence.

% HIV positive agents who never receive treatment proceed through acute, chronic, and late-stage infection with associated changes in viral load, which affects the probability of transmitting HIV, and CD4 counts, which impact  mortality. Progression to late-stage infection is indicated by a CD4 count <200 which occurs an average of 8 years after infection in the absence of treatment. Treatment with ART modifies this trajectory by extending the chronic stage (i.e., slowing disease progression) and reducing the probability of death. Sexual behaviors and engagement in HIV prevention and care (e.g., PrEP use, frequency of testing, time from diagnosis to antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation, and viral suppression) can vary according to agent characteristics, including demographics and social determinants of health, depending on availability of data, as described below.

% \subsection{uConnect study}
% \label{sec:supp-uconnect}
% Input parameter values for sexual behaviors, network characteristics, and demographics were derived from the uConnect study, a longitudinal study of 618 Black sexual minority men and transgender women conducted in Chicago between 2013 and 2015. \cite{schneider_at_2017, schneider_skaathun_2017, khanna_preexposure_2016} Participants were recruited using respondent driven sampling (RDS)\cite{heckathorn_respondent-driven_1997, magnani_review_2005} over 3 waves of data collection and followed for an average of 18 months after the baseline study visit. Retention was 85\% at 9 months and 82\% at 18 months. uConnect examined the impact of social influences on HIV transmission risk and use of biomedical and behavioral prevention among young Black MSM and TGW residing on the South Side of Chicago, an area of the city burdened by lack of resources and high HIV prevalence. Sampling, recruitment, and data collection have been previously described.\cite{schneider_skaathun_2017} Briefly, recruits (seeds) were selected from community organizations, health clinics, and virtual spaces such as Facebook. Eligibility criteria included self-identifying as African American or Black; residing or spending the majority of time on the South Side of Chicago; having been assigned male sex at birth; being between the ages of 16 and 29; reporting oral or anal sex with a male partner in the past 24 months; and being willing and able to provide informed consent. At each study visit, participants provided information on sociodemographics, substance use, sexual behaviors, HIV prevention and care engagement, and characteristics of their social and sexual networks. Analyses to generate the model parameters used relevant time points (baseline visit, average across visits, or changes over time) depending on the availability of specific variables and parameters of interest.

% Separable temporal exponential-family random graph models (STERGMs) were used to represent the dynamic sexual networks. STERGMs extend exponential-family random graph models (ERGMs) -- which represent network structure through the presence or absence of ties fit to target statistics describing an observed network -- by modeling tie formation and dissolution as separate but jointly estimated processes.\cite{krivitsky_separable_2014} We estimated separate STERGMs for the main and casual networks. Participants classified partners as main, casual, or exchange; exchange and casual partners were combined for estimation. Main and casual networks were modeled separately, with no constraint on overlap between them.

% For each network, the formation model gives the probability that a non-existing tie forms at time $t$, and the persistence model the probability that an existing tie is retained, each as a logistic function of a vector of network statistics with coefficients $\theta$:
% \begin{align}
% \operatorname{logit} P\!\left(Y_{ij,t}=1 \mid Y_{ij,t-1}=0,\, Y^{c}_{ij,t}\right) &= \theta^{+\prime}\,\delta\!\left(g^{+}(y)\right), \\
% \operatorname{logit} P\!\left(Y_{ij,t}=1 \mid Y_{ij,t-1}=1,\, Y^{c}_{ij,t}\right) &= \theta^{-\prime}\,\delta\!\left(g^{-}(y)\right),
% \end{align}
% where $Y_{ij,t}$ indicates a tie between agents $i$ and $j$ at time $t$, $Y^{c}_{ij,t}$ denotes the rest of the network, $\delta(\cdot)$ is the vector of change statistics, and the superscripts $+$ and $-$ denote the formation and persistence models (fit separately for main and casual networks).

% Formation statistics included the cross-sectional degree distribution (the proportion of agents with 0, 1, or $\geq 2$ ties at a given time), age homophily (the absolute age difference within a dyad), and a nodefactor term allowing agents who use substances to have more partners. The persistence model included mean partnership duration as an offset and assumed a homogeneous dissolution process, so that average partnership duration was constant across ties and did not vary with nodal characteristics. Coefficients were estimated by Markov chain Monte Carlo using the \texttt{statnet} suite of R packages\cite{handcock_statnet_2008, goodreau_statnet_2008} and passed to the simulation via the \texttt{rpy2} Python--R interface. Because networks are updated daily, partnerships can form and dissolve within a single day, representing one-time encounters; zero-duration partnerships were therefore retained when computing empirical durations and concurrency.

\subsection{Morris Screening} 

Due to the limitations of existing data, the values for multiple model parameters were uncertain. We explored plausible ranges for these parameters based on empirical data and existing literature as part of the calibration process to select the most likely set of parameter values that approximated the target HIV prevalence. Because of the large number of parameters, we were not able to conduct full sensitivity analysis on all of them. Therefore we selected the most uncertain parameters (i.e., those for which there was limited data or existing data was considered imprecise) and those considered to have a large impact on model outcomes (eg transmission probability), which resulted in 21 parameters for Morris screening.

Before model calibration, we performed a dimension reduction using the Morris screening method~\cite{morris1991factorial, campolongo2007effective} to identify the most influential parameters among the 21 uncertain model inputs. In the Morris screening method, elementary effects are computed, and the model inputs are ranked according to their influence on model outputs while accounting for potential nonlinearities and interactions. Briefly, the approach perturbs each parameter one at a time across a discretized grid of the input space, while randomly sampling the remaining parameters within their respective ranges. For each parameter \(x_i\), the corresponding elementary effect is computed as
\begin{equation}
    EE_i = \frac{Y(\mathbf{x}_{1}, \ldots, x_i + \Delta, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_{k}) - Y(\mathbf{x})}{\Delta},
\end{equation}
where \(Y(\cdot)\) denotes the model output of interest (in our case, the average HIV prevalance), and \(\Delta\) is a predetermined step size within the normalized \([0,1]\) input space. Repeating this process over multiple random trajectories yields a distribution of elementary effects for each parameter. Two summary statistics are typically derived: (i) the mean of the absolute elementary effects, \(\mu^*_i\), which reflects the overall influence of parameter \(i\) on the output, and (ii) the standard deviation, \(\sigma_i\), which captures nonlinearity and interaction effects. Parameters with large \(\mu^*_i\) are considered highly influential, whereas those with large \(\sigma_i\) indicate complex effects or interactions.

We implemented the Morris design using \(r = 20\) trajectories and \(p = 6\) grid levels. Based on the joint examination of \(\mu^*_i\) and \(\sigma_i\) values, six parameters were identified as key drivers of the model outputs and retained for subsequent calibration. The selected parameters and their ranges are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:morris_params}, while Figure~\ref{fig:morris_results} shows the corresponding sensitivity indices.

\subsection{Bayesian Calibration}
\label{sec:sbi}

Following Morris screening, the reduced six parameter INFORM-HIV model was calibrated to reproduce the observed HIV prevalence. Let $\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^6$ denote the retained parameter vector. For each parameter setting, the stochastic simulator was evaluated using multiple independent random seeds to account for intrinsic simulation variability. Annual HIV prevalence was recorded from each simulation, and the calibration summary statistic was defined as the average prevalence over the final five simulated years. The observed calibration target was the corresponding empirical five-year average HIV prevalence, $y_{\mathrm{obs}}=0.294$.

Calibration was performed using simulation-based inference~\cite{dyer2024black} with sequential neural posterior estimation (NPE). A prior distribution was specified over the reduced parameter space, and the simulator was treated as an implicit generative model that maps parameter values to the prevalence summary statistic through forward simulation. NPE learns an approximation to the posterior distribution $\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid y_{\mathrm{obs}})$, which characterizes parameter values consistent with the observed prevalence.

Training data were generated using a Latin hypercube design with $N=100$ parameter settings over the reduced parameter space. At each design point, the INFORM-HIV model was simulated $R=10$ times using independent random seeds, resulting in 1{,}000 simulations. The same set of random seeds was used across all parameter settings to reduce Monte Carlo variability when comparing simulator outputs. The resulting parameter-summary pairs were used to train the sequential NPE model. After training, 100 parameter vectors were sampled from the approximate posterior and used as calibrated parameter sets for the subsequent scenario analysis.

\section{ART and PrEP Reduction Scenario Exploration}\label{sec:supp-surrogate-scenario-exploration}

Following calibration, 100 posterior samples were retained as checkpointed model states at the end of the 20-year calibration period. Each checkpoint was propagated forward for an additional 10 years under intervention settings defined by percent reductions in ART coverage and PrEP use, $\mathbf{u}=(r_{\mathrm{ART}},r_{\mathrm{PrEP}})\in\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^2$, relative to the baseline. Direct evaluation over a dense intervention grid is computationally expensive because every intervention must be simulated for each checkpointed model state.

To accelerate scenario exploration, a Gaussian process (GP) surrogate~\cite{gramacy_surrogates_2020} was constructed for each checkpoint. For a fixed checkpoint, let $\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{R}^{T}$ denote the projected prevalence or incidence trajectory over the next $T=10$ years. The trajectory was represented using a low-dimensional basis expansion

\[\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{u}) \approx \Phi\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{u}),
\]
where $\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times J}$ contains basis vectors obtained from singular value decomposition and $\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{R}^{J}$ denotes the corresponding basis coefficients. Independent GP models were fitted to each coefficient, yielding the predictive distribution

\[
\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{u}) \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)} \sim
N\!\left(
\boldsymbol{\mu}_k(\mathbf{u}),
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k(\mathbf{u})
\right),
\]
where $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}$ denotes the $k$th posterior sample retained during calibration.

Training data for each surrogate consisted of an initial Latin hypercube design of 20 intervention combinations with 10 stochastic replicates per combination. Separate surrogate models were constructed for prevalence and incidence at each checkpoint.

The final predictive distribution was obtained by averaging over the posterior samples retained during calibration,

\[
p(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{u})\mid y_{\mathrm{obs}})
\approx
\frac{1}{K}
\sum_{k=1}^{K}
N\!\left(
\boldsymbol{\mu}_k(\mathbf{u}),
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k(\mathbf{u})
\right),
\]
where $K=100$, and $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k(\cdot), \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k(\cdot)$ are the mean and covariance of the fitted GP surrogate. This multi-surrogate formulation propagates uncertainty from both Bayesian calibration and surrogate prediction into the projected intervention outcomes.

%% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model Notation}

We model the relationship between annual U.S. government investment in HIV prevention and treatment and the resulting population-level coverage of ART and PrEP among MSM. The key quantities are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $P_{\text{ART}}$: proportion of HIV-positive MSM receiving ART
    \item $P_{\text{PrEP}}$: proportion of PrEP-eligible HIV-negative MSM using PrEP
    \item $G_{\text{ART}}$: annual government spending on ART allocated to MSM
    \item $G_{\text{PrEP}}$: annual government spending on PrEP allocated to MSM
    \item $C_{\text{ART}}$: annual per-person cost of ART (2025 USD)
    \item $C_{\text{PrEP}}$: annual per-person cost of PrEP (2025 USD)
    \item $N_{\text{HIV+}}$: number of HIV-positive MSM in the United States
    \item $N_{\text{HIV-}}$: number of PrEP-eligible HIV-negative MSM in the United States
    \item $\alpha$: ART program inefficiency factor (accounts for delayed linkage,
          suboptimal adherence, and administrative overhead)
    \item $\beta$: PrEP program inefficiency factor (accounts for drop-off and
          adherence losses)
    \item $\gamma_{\text{ART}}$: proportion of HIV-positive MSM accessing ART through
          non-governmental sources (e.g., private insurance)
    \item $\gamma_{\text{PrEP}}$: proportion of PrEP-eligible MSM accessing PrEP through
          non-governmental sources
\end{itemize}

\subsection{U.S. Government Expenditure Estimates}

Total federal spending on HIV care and treatment in FY 2022 was approximately \$28.7 billion (base) plus \$6.7 billion (discretionary) = \$35.4 billion for
ART-related programs, and \$7.1 billion (base) plus \$1.0 billion (discretionary) = \$8.1 billion for PrEP-related programs~\cite{KFF2023}.

To allocate these national figures to the MSM population, we use two epidemiological proportions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item MSM account for approximately $52.4\%$ of HIV-positive individuals in the
          United States: $638{,}900$ MSM out of $1{,}218{,}400$ total

    \item MSM account for approximately $70.0\%$ of adults with PrEP indications
          in the United States: $851{,}240$ MSM out of $1{,}216{,}210$ eligible
          adults (2018)~\cite{prepcosts2018}.
\end{itemize}

These proportions are national U.S. estimates covering all MSM regardless of
race/ethnicity and all adult age groups.

\begin{align}

\paragraph{PrEP cost ($C_{\text{PrEP}}$)}

\subsection{Calibration of Inefficiency and Substitution Parameters}

At the observed baseline (current funding and coverage), the inefficiency parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are solved from the coverage equations so

\subsection{Coverage Predictions Under Funding Reductions}

Let $\Delta \in [0,1]$ denote a proportional funding reduction, so that government spending under the reduced scenario is $(1-\Delta)\,G$. Coverage under a funding reduction is then:

\begin{align}
P_{\text{ART}}(\Delta)  &= \min\!\left(1,\;
    \frac{(1-\Delta)\,G_{\text{ART}}}{\alpha\, C_{\text{ART}}\, N_{\text{HIV+}}}
    + \gamma_{\text{ART}}\right), \label{eq:supp-PART-delta} \\[6pt]
P_{\text{PrEP}}(\Delta) &= \min\!\left(1,\;
    \frac{(1-\Delta)\,G_{\text{PrEP}}}{\beta\, C_{\text{PrEP}}\, N_{\text{HIV-}}}
    + \gamma_{\text{PrEP}}\right). \label{eq:supp-PPrEP-delta}
\end{align}

The privately financed component $\gamma$ is unaffected by $\Delta$; only the publicly financed share of coverage declines. The $\min(1,\cdot)$ cap ensures that coverage cannot exceed 100\%.

For example, a 50\% reduction in government ART spending reduces ART coverage by less than
50\%; the actual decline is $(1 - \gamma_{\text{ART}})\times 50\% \approx 42.5$~percentage
points starting from baseline, before the min constraint is applied.

\subsection{Limitations of the Linear Approximation and Directions for Future Work}

The main paper adopts a linear formulation for the mapping from government spending to ART and PrEP coverage, chosen for parsimony and because the true empirical relationship between aggregate funding and coverage is not well-identified at the scale of the reductions considered. This section elaborates on the sources of nonlinearity that the linear model abstracts away, and outlines how future work could incorporate them.

\paragraph{Why the linear model is a first-order approximation.} In practice, the true response of coverage to funding changes is likely nonlinear. Large funding cuts may induce threshold effects such as clinic closures, workforce contraction, or supply-chain disruptions, which would amplify reductions in coverage
beyond what a proportional model predicts. Moreover, the impact of funding changes is heterogeneous across insurance and socioeconomic strata: privately insured individuals are largely insulated from short-term changes in public funding, while uninsured populations --- who depend most heavily on Ryan White/ADAP and Medicaid --- are more directly and severely affected. Neither of these mechanisms is captured by the constant-parameter linear model.

\paragraph{Three nonlinear mechanisms.} Three concrete sources of nonlinearity are abstracted away in the current model:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Insurance heterogeneity.} MSM are stratified by insurance status
    (private, Medicaid, uninsured). A uniform percentage reduction in government
    spending produces highly unequal impacts across strata: privately insured
    individuals are largely insulated (high private substitution floor $\gamma$),
    while uninsured individuals face steep declines (low $\gamma$, high
    $\alpha$/$\beta$). The aggregate population-level response is therefore
    nonlinear even though each stratum responds linearly to its allocated funding.
    An illustrative calculation suggests that a 30\% budget cut might reduce ART
    coverage among the privately insured by only 2--3 percentage points, Medicaid
    enrollees by 15--20 points, and the uninsured by 40--50 points --- yielding an
    aggregate decline substantially larger than the naive linear prediction.

    \item \textbf{Threshold and infrastructure collapse.} Healthcare delivery systems
    carry fixed costs (staffing, facilities, equipment). Sufficiently large funding
    cuts can trigger clinic closures or workforce losses that amplify coverage
    declines beyond linear predictions once spending falls below a structural
    threshold (estimated at roughly $65$--$75\%$ of baseline in related
    literature). Below this threshold, the effective inefficiency of the remaining
    system rises sharply as economies of scale are lost.

    \item \textbf{Dynamic inefficiency.} The parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are
    held constant across all funding levels in the current model. In reality,
    large cuts are likely to increase inefficiency (loss of economies of scale,
    higher administrative overhead per patient), while funding expansions may
    improve it. Allowing $\alpha$ and $\beta$ to vary with spending level would
    capture this feedback.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Implications for the current analysis.}
For the funding reductions considered in the main analysis (up to $75\%$ of baseline), the linear model likely understates coverage losses among uninsured and publicly insured MSM while providing a reasonable approximation for privately insured subpopulations. Overall population-level estimates should therefore be interpreted as conservative: true coverage losses could be larger if threshold effects or insurance-heterogeneity mechanisms are operative. Sensitivity of results to the choice of $\gamma$, $\alpha$, and $\beta$ can be partially assessed by varying these parameters within the ranges reported in Table~\ref{tab:supp-param-estimates}. Table~\ref{tab:supp-r-sensitivity} reports this directly for $\gamma_{\text{PrEP}}$'s underlying payer-mix parameter $r$ at $r=\rLower$ (unvalidated placeholder pending AFC), $r=\rBaseline$ (base case), and $r=\rUpper$ (prior national estimate); $P^{0}=\PZeroPrEP$ is not varied separately, since $(P^{0}-\gamma_{\text{PrEP}})/P^{0}=1-r$ is algebraically independent of $P^{0}$.

\input{../04_cost_mapping/sensitivity/output/paper_sensitivity_table.tex}

\paragraph{Future work.}
Incorporating insurance stratification is the most tractable near-term extension: data on population shares by insurance status, stratum-specific substitution rates, and stratum-specific inefficiency factors are available from CDC National HIV Behavioral Surveillance, Ryan White Program Services Reports, and Medicaid enrollment records. Threshold and dynamic-inefficiency effects would require additional data on provider-level fixed costs and historical responses to funding shocks (e.g., sequestration impacts on Ryan White, state-level ADAP waiting lists). We defer these extensions to future work.
